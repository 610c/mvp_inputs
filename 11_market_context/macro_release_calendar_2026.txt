B. OIL — NOT CAPTURED, AND WHY
================================================================
The EIA release-schedule page (https://www.eia.gov/about/release_schedule.php)
returns FILE NOT FOUND — EIA redesigned its site and the schedule moved. I am
NOT going to write down EIA's usual cadence from memory and present it as
captured data; that is exactly the kind of plausible-looking fabrication this
corpus should not contain.

WHAT YOU ACTUALLY NEED FOR OIL, and it is not a calendar:
The 2026 oil story is event-driven, not release-driven. Every participant in
the window ties their inflation view to the Middle East conflict and the
Strait of Hormuz, and those are NEWS events on no schedule at all:
  - Collins (Boston, 8/25): "some degree of reopening of the Strait of
    Hormuz, two developments I am watching closely... oil inventories have
    dropped to historically low levels"
  - Paulson (Philadelphia, 8/4): "The June ceasefire in the Middle East
    provided temporary relief... Oil prices have since jumped and remain
    volatile"
  - Venable (Atlanta, 8/11): "A clear resolution to the war would likely
    get oil shipments moving more freely"
  - Daly (SF, 8/5): energy prices drive "the lion's share" of the change in
    consumer inflation expectations
  - April 2026 minutes: "the crude oil futures curve was higher than the
    curve prevailing at the time of the March FOMC meeting... remained
    steeply downward sloping"
A price series and a conflict-event timeline would serve the model far
better here than an EIA publication calendar. FLAGGED AS OUTSTANDING.

CONSEQUENCES FOR SCORING — these are the traps:

1. PAULSON (8/4) HAD NOT SEEN THE JULY CPI OR THE JULY PAYROLLS.
   Her essay cites "Headline PCE inflation eased to 3.7 percent in June"
   (released 30 July) and "Second quarter GDP increased a modest 1.5
   percent" (advance, 30 July). Everything she says is conditioned on data
   through end-July. She is NOT ignoring the August prints; they did not
   exist.

2. COLLINS (8/25) AND BARR (9/1) SAW THE JULY CPI AND PAYROLLS; PAULSON
   DID NOT. Collins refers to "June and July inflation reports were mildly
   encouraging" — she has one more month of information than Paulson.
   Comparing their hawkishness without adjusting for this compares two
   different information sets, not two different dispositions.

3. NOBODY IN THE WINDOW HAD SEEN THE AUGUST CPI. It releases 11 September,
   after the window closes and before the September FOMC. Every hawkish
   conditional in this corpus — Paulson's, Collins's, Barr's — is explicitly
   waiting on a print that had not happened yet. Barr says so directly:
   "At our September FOMC meeting, we will again discuss the outlook."

4. THE 4 SEPTEMBER PAYROLLS PRINT LANDS ON THE WINDOW'S LAST DAY.
   Anything a participant said before 8:30 AM that morning was said without
   it. If the corpus is timestamped only to the day, 4 September is
   ambiguous and should be treated as pre-print unless a document says
   otherwise.

5. THE JANUARY 2026 DATA GAP IS REAL AND WILL BITE A PANEL.
   The Dec 2025 PCE print does not exist as its own release; Oct and Nov
   2025 were published together on 22 January 2026 and December on
   20 February. The January payrolls print came 11 February rather than the
   first Friday. This is government-shutdown residue and it is visible in
   the January minutes ("data collection issues related to the government
   shutdown had likely pushed down the levels of the CPI and the PCE price
   index in November and December").
